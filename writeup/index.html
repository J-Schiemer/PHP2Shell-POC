Welcome to Hotel Zend

The whole guide in one scene. Every later chapter reuses this cast.

The story ELI5 · continuous allegory

Hotel Zend is open all night. In this lab it has exactly one night clerk: Mira. She works at Desk 7, and she handles one guest at a time. Every guest is an HTTP request; Mira is the worker process that serves it.

Mira has two kinds of paper, and they behave very differently.

The Blotter is the stack of scratch paper Mira uses for the guest in front of her. It is huge, cheap, and temporary. She writes on it while the guest is there, and at checkout it is swept clean so the next guest can use the same paper. In code, the Blotter is the request's memory: the heap the engine fills while one request runs.

The Ledger is the little notebook in her pocket. It survives every checkout. When Mira needs to remember something between guests — a session, an error, a setting — she writes a line in the Ledger. Most lines look like an address: “the guest's session folder is on page 12.” In code, the Ledger is the worker's persistent state: the globals that outlive a request.

That is the whole setup, and one rule matters: the Ledger outlives the Blotter.

Now the problem. Suppose Mira writes “the guest's folder is on page 12,” and then the Blotter is wiped. The note still says page 12. That page has been erased, reused, or handed to someone else — and Mira has no way to tell. She trusts her own handwriting, because a Ledger line is supposed to be trustworthy.

So the next guest asks a small question, Mira looks up the old line, and follows it. If the page is blank, she reads nothing. If it has been reused, she may read the next guest's private data. If it contains something shaped like an instruction, she may follow it. That last case is how a memory-safety bug turns into command execution.

There is also a Session Drawer for coat-check tickets (session state), a Pink Slip for the last fatal complaint (an error backtrace), and an Inspector who arrives to test whether the hotel has really fixed its lock (the checker). They appear later. For now, all you need is Mira, the Blotter, and the Ledger.

The Ledger outlives the Blotter. Most of this story is what happens when someone forgets to erase a line.

Go deeper scope, provenance, and what “request memory” really means

PHP's request allocator is ZendMM. It carves 2 MB chunks from the OS, then serves small allocations from bins and pages inside those chunks. At the end of a request, shutdown_memory_manager(..., full=0) resets the main chunk and moves other chunks to a cache. The memory is not returned to the OS immediately; it is marked free and reused by the next request on the same worker.

That reuse is the point. A stale pointer into a freed request page is not necessarily unmapped. It often points at a perfectly readable chunk of heap that now belongs to somebody else — which is exactly why the leak works, and why the crash is a double free rather than a clean segfault.

This guide covers the two paths implemented by this repository: the 8.4 mod_php OOM + stale-session chain and the 8.5 mod_php fatal-backtrace + early-INI crash oracle. The advisories describe more variants — FPM, FrankenPHP, LSAPI, OPcache type confusion, application-specific producers — that the shipped checker does not claim to prove. The scope page is explicit about that. All addresses quoted here come from recorded QEMU linux/amd64 lab runs; native x86_64 uses different ranges, and even the chunk-relative offsets are image- and allocator-specific.

Provenance: advisories GHSA-w8hw-w9fx-j79w and GHSA-6wmf-4rv5-p3rp; fix commit 3584a98bda; the public research PoC 9bie/php2shell-bypass-aslr-exp at 17e98ee8, referenced but not vendored. Chapter 02 · The machine

The hotel never closes

Prefork workers are reused on purpose. That efficiency is also the reason a forgotten pointer becomes a cross-request bug: the next guest gets the same clerk and the same notebook.

01

One clerk, many guests

Why the same process handles the producer and the consumer.

The story ELI5 · continuity

Hotels do not hire a new clerk for every guest. Hiring takes time, training takes longer, and Mira already knows the building, the phone numbers, and where the coffee is. So the hotel keeps her and gives her the next guest too. In code, this is prefork: a small pool of worker processes, each one reused for request after request.

At checkout Mira is meant to do three things, always in this order. First, finish any pending paperwork, so nothing is left half-written. Second, erase every Ledger line that points at this guest's Blotter. Third, and only then, wipe the Blotter clean.

The order matters. If she wipes first, she can no longer tell which lines were safe to erase. A line like “the session folder is on page 12” does not become harmless after the wipe; it becomes wrong, while still looking perfectly readable.

Most Ledger lines are harmless. “The front door code is 4411” does not point at the Blotter. But “the session folder is on page 12” does. If that line is still there after the wipe, the next guest can send Mira back to page 12. She will read it, free it, or make a decision based on it, because nothing on the line says “expired.”

In a busy hotel there are many Miras, so an attacker has to be lucky about which clerk gets the follow-up guest. In this lab there is exactly one Mira and KeepAlive Off, so the guest who writes the line and the guest who reads it are guaranteed to meet the same process. The lab removes the race, not the bug.

The same clerk is the feature. The same clerk is the vulnerability.

The mechanics the request lifecycle and the two heaps

The lab uses Apache prefork with mod_php. The parent process owns the listening socket and forks workers; each worker handles one request at a time. PHP's module startup (MINIT) has already run, so persistent structures exist before the first guest. Per-request work is bracketed by php_request_startup() and php_request_shutdown().

Accept: the parent hands over a connection

The prefork parent accepts the TCP connection and passes the socket to an idle worker. The worker parses the request line and headers. At this point no request-owned PHP memory exists yet; the worker is a warm process with persistent globals and a reused ZendMM heap.

Lab detail: MaxRequestWorkers is reduced to one and KeepAlive is off. The upstream public PoC instead keeps MaxRequestWorkers - 1 partial POSTs open to pin the four chain requests to one child.

Startup: the Blotter is opened

php_request_startup() initializes the executor, superglobals, input handling, and module RINIT hooks. Automatic session start and multipart body parsing happen here. For mod_php, .htaccess and php_value / php_admin_value settings are applied by apply_config() before this call. For FPM, init_request_info() applies PHP_VALUE / PHP_ADMIN_VALUE and parses HTTP_AUTHORIZATION before startup.

Why it matters: if startup fails, mod_php still calls shutdown. That is the 8.4 “faint” path. The pre-startup configuration window is also where the 8.5 consumer fires, before init_executor() has cleared the stale backtrace.

Execute: the guest does their business

PHP compiles and runs the script. Application code, extensions, session writes, and error handling all allocate from the request heap. A fatal error here — an uncaught exception, a parse failure, a timeout, an OOM, a failed require — can abandon cleanup halfway through.

Producer territory: this is where request-owned state is most often published into a worker global: a session id, a session decoder, an object, an error backtrace.

Shutdown: filing and erasing

php_request_shutdown() calls RSHUTDOWN hooks, runs destructors, writes sessions, flushes output, and calls module shutdown. This is the last chance to clear every persistent pointer that refers to request memory. The 8.4 chain targets destruction here; the 8.5 crash happens in a later request's error path, but the missed reset happens in this phase.

Reset: the Blotter is swept

shutdown_memory_manager(heap, full=0, ...) resets the main 2 MB chunk: free tail rewound, free pages reset, small-bin free lists cleared. Non-main chunks move to a cache. The next request's allocations start from the beginning of the chunk, but old pointers are still numerically valid and may still contain old bytes.

This is the window: after the sweep and before the next request's initialization, the Ledger can still contain a pointer into the Blotter. Any consumer that runs in that gap reads or frees reused memory.

Who owns what. The distinction is not “old” versus “new” memory; it is lifetime. The Blotter is per-request. The Ledger is per-worker. A pointer may only cross the boundary if its target also crosses the boundary.

The invariant, stated once

Before the Blotter is swept, every Ledger line that points into it must be erased, and every pending destructor that will touch it must have run. The two CVEs are two different ways to violate that invariant: one interrupts the erasing, one repopulates a line after the erasing.

Go deeper ZendMM, fast shutdown, and why the heap is reusable

Chunks, pages, bins. ZendMM requests 2 MB chunks from the OS (ZEND_MM_CHUNK_SIZE). Each chunk is divided into 4 KB pages; small allocations are served from per-size bins, large ones get dedicated runs of pages. The “main chunk” is the one the allocator prefers for the request. At request shutdown with full=0, the main chunk is reset in place, non-main chunks are parked in a cache, and the allocator's free tail returns to ZEND_MM_FIRST_PAGE. That is why a new request's first 56-byte allocation usually lands near the start of the chunk, while a producer's late allocation can be hundreds of pages in — the structural gap that blocks the 8.5 groom.

Fast shutdown. The execution path distinguishes a fast request shutdown from a full one. In 8.5.11, zend_shutdown_executor_values() has a block guarded by if (!fast_shutdown) that destroys and undefines EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace). The fast path skips both. The heap is still reset afterwards. The pointer survives; the memory does not.

Why one worker is a lab simplification. With many workers, the attacker cannot choose which process gets the consumer. The upstream PoC pins a child by holding all but one worker busy with partial POSTs. This repo removes that race entirely for deterministic evidence. It does not make the bug less real; it makes the demonstration repeatable.

Why the addresses look like 0x400…. The lab ran linux/amd64 images under QEMU user-mode emulation on Apple Silicon. Guest mappings land in a low range. Native x86_64 typically shows 0x7f… pointers. The parser never assumes either range; it derives libphp from a known symbol offset and clusters the rest by 2 MB.

01

The unlabeled crate

A request that looks small at the front door and enormous at the desk.

The story ELI5 · continuity

A guest arrives with a crate. The front door has a rule: crates over 8 units are refused. But this crate has no label saying how big it is, and the rule only reads labels. So the guest is waved through. In code, this is a request without a usable Content-Length, so the post_max_size check has nothing to measure.

At the desk, the crate keeps unfolding. Every form inside contains more forms, and every folder contains another folder. The paper is thin in the crate but thick once Mira has to lay it all out: names inside names inside names, thousands of empty file folders, each one needing a little cover sheet, plus a few heavy binders for weight. On the wire it looked small. In memory it is enormous.

Mira keeps unpacking until she reaches the 128-unit desk limit. A normal clerk would say “stop, I am out of room,” put the forms down, and finish tidying up. Mira instead faints. The hotel calls this a bailout: the work stops instantly and jumps straight to the end of the shift, skipping everything in between.

What it skips is the cleanup. The hotel calls the cleanup crew anyway, and the crew sweeps the Blotter clean. When Mira comes to, one Ledger line is still there: “session folder, page 12.” Mira does what the line says. She goes to page 12 and files whatever she finds there, because nobody ever erased the line.

Page 12 is no longer hers. The paper has been wiped and possibly reused. It may be blank. It may hold another guest's data. Or it may hold something shaped like a folder cover that says “run this.” Mira cannot tell the difference, because the line told her this page was still hers.

A faint is not a tidy checkout. The Blotter gets swept; the Ledger keeps its lines.

The mechanics what longjmp does to cleanup

In the vulnerable design, an allocation failure inside a bailout-capable region does not return NULL. It calls zend_bailout(), which uses longjmp() to jump to the nearest zend_try / zend_catch frame. The jump skips the normal return path, including any cleanup that was supposed to run between the failure point and the catch.

/* schematic, not the literal source */
if (UNEXPECTED(allocation_failed())) {
    zend_bailout();          /* longjmp: no unwinding, no destructors */
}
/* ...cleanup that now never runs... */

The advisory's key sentence is stronger than “a failed allocation”: request memory is temporarily reachable before it is fully initialized. If any allocation fails at that point, PHP does not properly account for the early exit and leaves the request memory in an invalid state. The failed allocation does not need to be related to the invalid memory.

On Apache mod_php, request startup is followed by request shutdown unconditionally, even when startup has failed. The shutdown procedure therefore processes the invalid state. On FPM, a failed php_request_startup() exits the child, which removes that particular sequence; the advisory still considers FPM susceptible in other contexts after a successful startup.

How to reach OOM while staying inside the apparent limits

The advisory lists several multipart strategies. They share a theme: the wire representation and the in-memory representation have different sizes, and the front-door checks do not always measure the expensive one.

Strategy 1

No usable content length

A chunked multipart request without a usable declared content length can pass the initial post_max_size check and keep supplying decoded body data. The entry check that assumes a length is skipped.

Strategy 2

Headers that expand

A single multipart part can carry an unrestricted number of headers. Representing those headers inside PHP — hash entries, zvals, strings — costs substantially more than their bytes on the wire.

Strategy 3

Free folders, real metadata

Empty file controls do not count toward max_file_uploads, but they still create the normal file metadata arrays. Nested field names multiply the generated array work without adding much body size.

Strategy 4

Upload-progress cleanup

Session upload progress can put session handling on the startup path before ordinary session initialization. OOM during that cleanup leaves session globals pointing at request memory that is about to be freed.

The image starts from stock php.ini-production and overrides only the session, upload, and input limits below. The stock values for memory_limit, post_max_size, and display_errors are part of the calibration.

Why this is not “just a DoS”

A crash is the minimum. The real problem is that shutdown acts on memory whose ownership has already changed. Combined with the stale-state bug in the next chapter, that gives an attacker a way to turn “invalid state” into a read, then into a controlled call.

Go deeper bailout semantics, the exact advisory, and the mitigation trap

What “invalid state” means here. The request heap is not corrupted in the sense of arbitrary bytes being overwritten. It is incompletely initialized. Some structures are reachable from globals before their constructor has finished, or before the request is marked as failed. A later shutdown routine walks them and branches on fields that were never set, or invokes destructors for objects whose storage was never fully published. The exact failures are implementation details; the ownership violation is the invariant.

Bailout is not exception unwinding. C does not run destructors during longjmp(). PHP's zend_try block catches the jump, but anything between the allocation and the jump target is skipped. That is acceptable when the catch path can rebuild state. It is not acceptable when the request is already partially published to persistent globals.

The mitigation trap. The advisory explicitly warns that setting memory_limit=-1 is incomplete: if mmap() or another underlying allocation fails, PHP can still reach longjmp(). It also says not to use RLIMIT_AS or RLIMIT_DATA, because those make mmap() fail and trigger the vulnerable path. Conservative post_max_size, frontend enforcement of decoded body size, lower nesting, bounded parts/headers/uploads, blocking multipart when unused, and a cgroup memory limit are all partial hardening measures — not a substitute for the patch.

Fix design, previewed. Commit 3584a98bda removes longjmp() for OOM. A delayed, graceful soft OOM is recorded in executor globals and raised at the next VM safe point; crossing a second, higher threshold performs an immediate hard OOM with _exit(), so shutdown handlers never run on invalid state. New fallible allocation APIs let callers return NULL instead of bailing out. The broader state-reset work is the other half of the fix, and it is why a partial backport that only changes longjmp() is not complete.

Metadata. Published 2026-10-21; CVSS v4.0 9.2; affected < 8.2.35, < 8.3.36, < 8.4.27, < 8.5.12; fixed in those four releases. The 8.4 chain in this repo uses the OOM during mod_php multipart startup as its producer.

01

Two guests, one notebook

The producer publishes; the consumer consumes. They never meet.

The story ELI5 · continuity

Mira has a rule for the Ledger: write in pencil, erase at checkout. Most nights it works. But there are two ways to get it wrong, and they are worth keeping separate.

Way one: a guest interrupts her before she erases. A form is malformed, a required page is missing, or she runs out of time. The hotel calls any of these a fatal error. She drops the pencil, the shift ends immediately, and the line remains exactly as it was. The Blotter is swept anyway, because the hotel never skips the sweep.

Way two: she does erase the line, but then a late complaint arrives — a warning from the cleanup crew, an extension shutting down, a setting that was rejected. That complaint writes a new line in the same place, after the erasing is done. The notebook now describes the room that has just been cleared.

Either way, the next guest arrives before Mira has opened the desk for the day. The guest asks a small question — “what is my session name?”, “what does this setting mean?” — and the answer requires reading the Ledger. Mira trusts the old line. She reads, frees, or branches on a page that no longer belongs to her. The guest never has to break in; the clerk does the reading for them.

This is why the bug has two roles. A producer is a request that writes the line and does not clean it up. A consumer is a later request that reads the line before normal startup has replaced it. The two guests do not need to know each other. They only need the same Mira.

A producer writes the line. A consumer trusts it. The Blotter in between belongs to nobody.

The mechanics the five-step model from the advisory
1 · Producera reachable operation stores request state in a global → 2 · Missed resetfatal abandons it, or a late diagnostic repopulates it → 3 · Freerequest memory is released; the global still points in → 4 · Reusethe same worker takes another request → 5 · Consumerpre-init code reads, frees, or branches on it

The advisory names two broad classes of consumer. Both run before normal request initialization has a chance to replace the stale value.

Consumer A

INI diagnostics

A warning, notice, or deprecation raised while applying per-request INI settings. It does not need to be fatal, displayed, or logged. The error machinery reads global error state before it decides whether to report anything.

Reachability: mod_php applies .htaccess / php_value / php_admin_value in apply_config() before php_request_startup(). FPM applies PHP_VALUE / PHP_ADMIN_VALUE in init_request_info() before startup. Ordinary php.ini is startup configuration and is not reapplied as a per-request consumer.

Consumer B

Session upload progress

Upload progress can reach session handling before ordinary session initialization. It can therefore consume session or decoder state left by an interrupted request, independently of the INI-diagnostic path.

Why it matters here: this is the consumer used by the 8.4 chain. A1 leaves the session state dangling; B1 and B2 reach it before session initialization would have replaced it.

Producers: how the line gets written

  • A fatal error interrupts cleanup. Parse and compile failures, uncaught errors, failure of require, Maximum execution time exceeded, and OOM can all abandon an operation before it performs its matching reset.
  • OOM after publication. If error, exception, object, or extension state was already published, the OOM does not need to be the same operation that published it.
  • A late diagnostic repopulates. A nonfatal warning from extension or server cleanup during a later shutdown stage can write new request-owned error information back to the global state after the normal reset.

Consumers: why the question arrives so early

The dangerous property is not “warning” or “session.” It is ordering. The consumer runs in the window after the previous request's heap is gone and before the current request's normal initialization has rebuilt the relevant global. In mod_php, per-directory configuration is applied in that window. In FPM, request-info parsing — authorization and PHP_VALUE — happens there too.

Why hiding errors does not help

Global state is read before PHP decides whether to report, display, or log the warning. Changing error_reporting, using @, or disabling display_errors or log_errors does not protect the path. A silent warning is still a read of stale state.

The confirmed 8.5 case, in one paragraph

With fatal_error_backtraces=1, an uncaught fatal error stores a backtrace array in request memory and leaves EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace) pointing at it. On 8.5.11 the fast shutdown path skips both the destructor and the ZVAL_UNDEF, and the request heap is reset. The next request's .htaccess php_value memory_limit <overflow> raises a diagnostic before init_executor(). zend_error_zstr_at() starts by destroying the stale zval, so PHP walks the freed backtrace array and double-frees it. That is the reliable VULNERABLE_CRASH oracle this repo ships.

    The mechanics chunked multipart + upload-progress OOM

    A1 is build_a1() in src/php2shell_check/payload.py. It sends a multipart body with Transfer-Encoding: chunked and no Content-Length. The body is a sequence of empty file controls whose names are deeply nested arrays:

    files[R00000KKKK…KKK][a][a]…[a]

    Depth is 64 and the key padding is 90 bytes. Each empty file still creates the normal upload metadata arrays, but empty controls do not count toward max_file_uploads. Thousands of these names multiply the hash-table work. The body then adds 14 wide fillers, 78 parts of 8 KB, 20 “burner” parts of 4 KB, and a PHP_SESSION_UPLOAD_PROGRESS field so the session path is active.

    During startup, PHP parses the multipart body and handles upload progress. The allocation that finally fails is not necessarily the one that owns the invalid state; it only has to happen while the session state is reachable and the request is not fully initialized. zend_bailout() jumps out. The session globals are not reset to match. The request heap is freed at shutdown.

    Why chunked matters: without a usable declared content length, the initial post_max_size entry check is skipped. The request can keep supplying decoded data while remaining within the apparent 8 MB limit. The expensive representation is built inside PHP, not on the wire.

    What the lab observes

    A1 typically returns HTTP 500 or closes the connection. The checker accepts 500 or a closed connection, waits 250 ms, then sends B1. If A1 returns anything else, the attempt is retried; the chain is not assumed to have worked.

    Go deeper payload constants, the front-door check, and why nested names amplify

    Default A1 shape: parts=860, depth=64, key_pad=90, fillers128=14, big=78, big_len=8000, burners=20, burner_len=4000, progress_len=4000, sid 00. The first two parts vary the Content-Type length (103 and 231 bytes) and filename to shape the early allocations; the rest use application/octet-stream with an empty filename. The builder is deliberately byte-exact against the public PoC, because the heap layout is allocation-sensitive.

    Why the front door misses it. The rfc1867_post_handler entry check wants a declared length. Chunked transfer has none. A frontend that enforces a decoded body-size limit independently is the advisory's recommended compensating control; relying on post_max_size alone is not enough.

    Why empty files still cost. Empty file controls are excluded from max_file_uploads but still produce the upload metadata structures. Nested field names (a[b][c]) force PHP to build nested arrays, so a small wire fragment can create a much larger in-memory graph. This is the “paper that expands” effect: the limit measures the crate, not the unfolded forms.

    What exactly dangles. The session module has published the session id and handler data in worker globals before the OOM. A normal shutdown would reset them. The bailout path does not complete that reset. The request heap is then reset by shutdown_memory_manager. PS(id) and PS(mod_data) still contain addresses in the freed heap.

    SAPI caveat. This exact A1 shape is a mod_php path because mod_php calls request shutdown after a failed startup. FPM exits the child when startup fails. The advisory's FPM-relevant variants publish state during application execution after a successful startup, then hit a fatal error and use upload progress as the early consumer. Those variants are not what this A1 builder exercises.

    The mechanics stale zend_string → oversized Set-Cookie

    B1 is build_b1(). It is another chunked multipart POST to /index.php with a small PHPSESSID, a PHP_SESSION_UPLOAD_PROGRESS field, and a calibrated sequence of page-sized values. The upload-progress handler reaches the session before ordinary session initialization — the consumer window from the previous chapter.

    The stale PS(id) is treated as a live zend_string. Its header and length live in request memory that has been freed and reused. The cookie encoder reads ZSTR_LEN(id) bytes and URL-encodes them into Set-Cookie: PHPSESSID=…. Because the stale length no longer matches the original one-byte id, the response contains far more than a session id. In the reference run it decodes to 819,208 bytes.

    B1's values are shaped to keep that walk productive: whole-page values with forged zend_string headers at chosen offsets, a 400-page fill that leaves a hole, then 900 nested arrays to occupy the hole. The exact layout is calibrated; the point is that the stale id walks a predictable chain of request blocks and the response carries adjacent worker memory instead of crashing immediately.

    The leak is parsed for two anchors:

    1. zval_ptr_dtor — a function pointer repeated throughout the leaked hash tables (1,095 occurrences in the reference layout). Subtracting its known offset from the observed pointer gives the libphp base.
    2. The main ZendMM chunk — pointers clustered by 2 MB. The cluster with the calibrated count (87 in the reference layout) is the main chunk; the fake array address S is a fixed offset from it.

    That is the ASLR bypass. B2 needs two absolute addresses: zif_system and S. B1 hands over both without brute force.

    Go deeper the leak spec, the parser's three proofs, and the portability caveat

    The leak spec. LEAK_SPEC is G:511:0x137 × 14, then G:400:0x137, G:511:0x137, then X:900. A G item writes pages*4096 − 25 bytes and forges a zend_string-shaped header at page*0x1000 − 0x1018: refcount/type, flags, length, and an 8-byte tag. The X item creates 900 nested fields named x%05d[a]…[a] to occupy the 111-page hole. Names are padded to 120 bytes; fake_len is 0xC8000.

    The parser's three proofs. leak.py first requires a Set-Cookie: PHPSESSID= and at least 64 KB of URL-decoded value. It scans every 8-byte word for a pointer q such that q − dtor_offset is page-aligned and positive; the most common candidate must occur at least 50 times. That derives libphp. It then clusters all other plausible pointers by q & ~0x1FFFFF, removes the id chunk and the libphp chunk, and picks the candidate whose count is closest to the calibrated 87, requiring at least 20 pointers. Only then does it compute S = main + 0xF8398 and zif_system = libphp + zif_offset.

    Why the parser is address-layout agnostic. It never assumes 0x7f… or 0x400…. It never assumes which cluster is “the id chunk”; it lets the counts decide. The two symbol offsets are read from the target's libphp.so ELF symbol table at runtime (with a known fallback for 8.4.26). The two heap constants — S_OFFSET = 0xF8398 and MAIN_BUCKET_COUNT = 87 — are calibrated for the official php:8.4.26-apache build. A candidate with a different allocator layout can fail the RCE stage for layout reasons; the leak oracle is independent of the symbol offsets and remains the portable check.

    The forged-string caveat. The B1 body is not a generic “read anything” primitive. It is a heap-shaping payload tuned to the reference build. If you change the image, the PHP version, the extension set, or the multipart limits, expect to re-calibrate. The checker pins exact multi-arch manifest digests for this reason; re-tagging or rebuilding the same PHP tag can move heap offsets.

    The mechanics cheap OOM, plain POST

    A2 is build_a2(): a normal (non-chunked) multipart POST with a 32-byte PHPSESSID, an upload-progress field, 115 small fillers, and 880 empty file controls nested to depth 64. It is smaller than A1 but still enough to hit OOM while the session verifier is live.

    The verifier is the session module's request-owned zval used to validate or clean up the session at shutdown. A normal shutdown would dtor it and clear the reference. The OOM abandons that path; the request heap is reset; the verifier remains reachable from worker globals and points into freed memory.

    Why a second OOM? B1's leak and the intervening request may consume or overwrite the state A1 left. A2 re-establishes a dangling session object immediately before B2, on the same single worker, after only a 250 ms pause. The chain is deliberately stateful: every request changes the desk for the next one.

    Go deeper builder defaults and why this is a separate stage

    Default shape: parts=880, depth=64, fillers=115, sid=b'a'*32, progress=b'k0'. The fillers come first, then the nested empty file controls. The field name pattern is the same files[R%05d…][a]… nesting used by A1, with no key padding.

    Why plain POST works. A2 does not need to bypass post_max_size; it only needs to exhaust memory during startup/session cleanup within the body it sends. The engine sends it with wire.post_plain, whereas A1 and B1 use wire.post_chunked. B2 also uses a plain POST because the spray lives in the parsed multipart fields.

    The stateful assumption. The engine runs A1 → B1 → A2 → B2 with 250 ms sleeps and a single worker. There is no locking beyond that; the design removes the multi-worker race rather than proving the chain under contention. The upstream PoC instead pins a child by occupying the other workers. If you add concurrency, expect to re-derive the timing and possibly see the chain fail for scheduling reasons, not because the bug is fixed.

    Failure mode. If A2 does not leave the verifier dangling, B2's spray has nothing to redirect and no proof file appears. The checker retries the whole sequence up to --attempts times (default 3) and reports FAILED rather than claiming success.

    The mechanics fake references, fake zend_array, ABI mismatch

    B2 is build_b2(s_addr, ht). It sends about 1,000 multipart values of 384 bytes, each containing a repeating 32-byte fake reference, plus one ht part containing the fake zend_array. The spray pattern is:

    offset 0x00 : refcount = 1
offset 0x04 : type     = 0x0A   (IS_REFERENCE)
offset 0x08 : value    = S      (the fake array)
offset 0x10 : val_type = 0x0107

    The dangling verifier is cleaned up at shutdown. Because the surrounding heap now looks like references to S, the cleanup follows the fake reference into the fake array. The fake array is a valid 56-byte zend_array header:

    OffsetFieldValue
    0x00GC refcount / type1 / IS_ARRAY
    0x08flags0x10 (static keys)

    At S + 0x20 the payload lays out a fake execute-data frame; at S + 0x80 a fake zend_string whose bytes are the command; at S + 0x98 the command itself. When zend_array_destroy() walks the array and calls pDestructor(&bucket_val), it is really calling zif_system(execute_data, return_value). The “bucket” pointer is interpreted as the execute data, and the fake string becomes argument zero. The ABI mismatch is the trick: a destructor signature is used as an internal-function call frame.

    The engine builds this with payload.build_ht(found.s_addr, found.zif_system, command) and a command of the form id > /out/<nonce>. The proof file contains:

    uid=33(www-data) gid=33(www-data) groups=33(www-data)

    The proof is written during shutdown, so the harness waits for the proof file rather than for a clean response. Destruction may abort the worker afterwards; the next attempt starts from a fresh container.

    Go deeper the exact fake array, offsets, and what the checker does with them

    Why a destructor call becomes code execution. zend_array_destroy() iterates the array's buckets and invokes pDestructor on each value. If the array header is attacker-controlled, pDestructor is an arbitrary function pointer. But its calling convention is void (*)(zval *). Internal PHP functions like zif_system have signature void (zend_execute_data *, zval *). The forged layout makes the first argument point at a fake execute frame, so zif_system reads its real argument from the frame instead of from the zval. That is why the payload needs the execute-data and zend_string layout, not just a function pointer.

    Why S is where it is. S_OFFSET = 0xF8398 is calibrated so that the fake array lands in the main ZendMM chunk at an address the reference build keeps stable. It is not a symbol; it is a heap-shape constant. The public PoC documents the same offset for the official image. main + 0xF8398 is computed only after the leak parser has chosen the main cluster and verified its pointer count.

    Why the offsets are read at runtime. zif_system and zval_ptr_dtor are ELF symbols in libphp.so. The checker reads them from the target image, so a different build can still work if the rest of the heap layout matches. For php:8.4.26-apache the known fallbacks are zif_system = libphp + 0x465370 and zval_ptr_dtor = libphp + 0x65EB60. The 8.5 values are different (0x5996e0 and 0x7b47a0 for the mod_php debug image), which is why an 8.4 profile cannot be reused blindly.

    What the spray is doing. The multipart field values are 384 bytes; the 32-byte fake reference repeats every 32 bytes starting at offset 8. With roughly 1,000 fields, the heap is saturated with “reference to S” patterns. The dangling verifier can now be freed into a layout that looks like a reference, so its destructor follows S. The 500 + 495 fields bracket the single ht part in the request body; ordering matters for the heap.

    Failure modes. If the leak derives a wrong S or zif_system, B2 does not write a proof. If the candidate image has a different allocator layout, the spray may not overlap the dangling verifier. The checker therefore insists on a vulnerable control producing the proof first; a candidate that fails to reproduce gets PATCHED only if the control was actually vulnerable. Otherwise the result is INCONCLUSIVE. That is the subject of the next two chapters.

    Why the chain works

    Four conditions have to line up. Remove any one and the chain stops — which is also why the checker's control matters.

    Condition 1

    Same worker

    The producer and every consumer must be handled by the same process, because the stale pointer lives in worker globals. The lab uses one prefork worker and KeepAlive Off; the upstream PoC pins a child with partial POSTs.

    Condition 2

    A stale pointer that still looks valid

    The freed request page must remain readable and structurally plausible. ZendMM resets the chunk in place, so the bytes are still there; a clean unmapping would just crash.

    Condition 3

    A leak to defeat ASLR

    B2 needs absolute addresses. B1's oversized cookie leaks the library base and the main chunk, so the attacker does not brute-force the address space.

    Condition 4

    Controlled bytes at the stale slot

    B2's multipart spray and fake array put attacker-chosen data where the dangling verifier's destructor will follow it. The function pointer and its fake call frame are both attacker-controlled.

    01

    The slip under the desk mat

    A producer and a consumer that both run before the desk is open.

    The story ELI5 · continuity

    When a guest makes a fatal complaint, Mira writes the details on a Pink Slip and keeps it under the desk mat. The slip is meant to help her finish the shift. It is supposed to be destroyed at checkout, before the mat is lifted and the Blotter is swept.

    On 8.5.11 the fast checkout path skips that step. The mat is lifted, the Blotter is swept, and the Pink Slip stays where it is — still pointing at the old pages. Nothing about the slip looks broken. That is the problem.

    The next morning, a guest fills in an invalid form. The hotel processes form complaints before it opens the desk for the day, because it needs those settings to set the desk up. The complaint handler starts by picking up whatever is on the Pink Slip.

    On 8.5.11 the slip points at the swept Blotter. Mira walks the old pages and files them again — a double free. The allocator notices that the same block was released twice and aborts the worker: zend_mm_heap corrupted.

    On 8.5.12 the checkout code erases the slip without touching the dead page. The morning complaint finds a blank slip and moves on. Same guest, same form, no crash.

    The Pink Slip is not a leak. It is a live pointer to a page that was already swept.

    The mechanics producer, missed reset, consumer, double free
    Producer/fatal.php?mode=user_error with fatal_error_backtraces=1 → Missed resetfast shutdown skips dtor + ZVAL_UNDEF → Freerequest heap reset; pointer remains → Consumerinvalid php_value memory_limit warning pre-startup → Consequencestale array dtor → double free → SIGABRT

    On 8.5.11

    zend_shutdown_executor_values() has a fast path when ZendMM is active. The destructor and reset of EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace) sit inside if (!fast_shutdown). On the fast path both are skipped. The request heap is then reset by shutdown_memory_manager(..., full=0), but the global still holds a pointer into the freed heap.

    The next request can reach that pointer before init_executor(). In mod_php, the .htaccess php_value diagnostic is applied by apply_config() before request startup. zend_error_zstr_at() begins with:

    zval_ptr_dtor(&EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace));
ZVAL_UNDEF(&EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace));

    The stale array is still structurally intact: top-level header, arData, frame hash, args hash. zend_array_destroy() walks it and frees blocks that belong to the reset heap. That is a double free, not a controlled call. ZendMM detects the corrupted metadata and aborts.

    On 8.5.12

    The fix commit 3584a98bda adds, after zend_objects_store_free_object_storage():

    /* may be used by zend_error_zstr_at before init_executor */
ZVAL_UNDEF(&EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace));

    It clears the pointer without freeing it; the request heap is already gone. The next request sees IS_UNDEF and the pre-init dtor is a harmless no-op.

    What the lab observes

    Control on 8.5.11: PHP Fatal error: p2s-backtrace-producer, then zend_mm_heap corrupted and AH00052: child pid … exit signal Abort (6). With fatal_error_backtraces=0, or on 8.5.12 with it enabled, the worker stays alive and only the startup warning appears.

    Go deeper the fast-shutdown block, the array layout, and the crash oracle

It is pre-startup by construction: apply_config() happens before php_request_startup().     The instrumented proof

    The destructor can reach zif_system if the zval is controlled in-process.

    The story ELI5 · the line between proof and exploit

    To prove the Pink Slip could be dangerous, the lab temporarily gives Mira a forged Pink Slip. This is instrumented evidence, not an attack: the lab plants the slip itself, before the morning complaint is handled. A guest cannot do this; the lab can.

    The FPM pivot and the five-slot gap

    The story ELI5 · the lobby

    The mechanics init_request_info, auth_user, and the structural blocker

    Chapter 08 · The fix · commit 3584a98bda

    Three rules on the staff wall

    The fix is not one clever line. It is a change of policy: stop fainting, make allocation fallible, reset state before reuse, and close the hotel immediately if the second alarm rings.

    “Avoid state corruption via bailout” 2026-10-18 11,747 changed lines partial backport ≠ complete fix

    Story so far The bugs are ownership failures across a request boundary. The fix changes how OOM exits and, just as importantly, guarantees that persistent state cannot outlive the memory it points at.

    01

    The staff-room notice

    What each rule means for Mira and for the code.

    The story ELI5 · continuity

    The hotel posts three rules in the staff room, plus one quieter note for the people who build the desk. Each rule removes a different part of the failure, and together they replace the old checkout policy rather than patching one function.

    Rule one: erase the line before you sweep. Every Ledger line that points at the Blotter must be cleared before the Blotter is wiped. If the sweep has already happened, clear the line anyway — do not touch the dead page. This is the 8.5 ZVAL_UNDEF: the Pink Slip is crossed out without being filed.

    Rule two: do not faint. Running out of desk is a normal event, not an emergency exit. Stop at the next safe checkpoint, record “out of memory” in the Ledger, and let the normal path finish cleaning up. Cleanup gets to finish, and the request fails without abandoning its own state. This is the soft OOM: deferred to a VM safe point instead of a longjmp().

    Rule three: if the second alarm rings, close the hotel immediately. If memory use crosses a second, higher threshold, do not try to be graceful. Shut the process down with _exit() so no shutdown handler can walk invalid state. Losing one worker is cheaper than letting it file from a dead page. This is the hard OOM.

    And a fourth, quieter rule: if an allocation can fail, let the caller decide. Do not turn every failure into a jump. The caller can free partial work and return an error through the normal path. That is the fallible-allocation API.

    The fix is a policy: no jumps through cleanup, no shutdown on invalid state, no stale lines.

    The mechanics the four design changes
    Change 01

    Remove longjmp() for OOM

    OOM no longer bails out of the current context. A delayed, graceful soft OOM is stored in executor globals and triggered at the next VM safe point. Cleanup can finish; the request can fail without abandoning its own state.

    Change 02

    Hard memory limit

    If consumption crosses a second, higher threshold, PHP performs an immediate hard OOM with _exit(). Shutdown handlers do not run on invalid state. The process is gone, but it cannot be tricked into filing from a dead page.

    Change 03

    Fallible allocations

    New femalloc / fecalloc / ferealloc / safe_femalloc APIs let callers return NULL instead of bailing out. A parser can report failure and unwind normally.

    Change 04

    State reset before reuse

    Globals reachable during request startup are reset, covering error, session, executor, object, and extension state. This is the other half of the fix — the half that actually closes CVE-2026-109619.

    Three exits, compared

    Mira: faints mid-task. The jump skips the cleanup between the failure and the catch. The Blotter is swept; the Ledger keeps its lines.

    Code: zend_bailout() / longjmp() to the nearest zend_try. No C unwinding, no destructors, no guarantee that partially published state is consistent. If startup later fails, mod_php still calls shutdown.

    Ledger: stale by construction. This is the vulnerable policy.

    Mira: puts the pencil down at the next safe checkpoint, writes “out of memory” in the Ledger, and finishes the shift in an orderly way.

    Code: the OOM is recorded in executor globals and raised at a VM safe point. Allocation callers can observe failure. Cleanup runs on a consistent state; the request fails rather than jumping through its own invariants.

    Ledger: reset before reuse. Stale pointers are cleared, not abandoned.

    Mira: the second alarm rings. The hotel closes immediately. No checkout, no filing, no chance to touch a bad page.

    Code: if memory crosses a second, higher threshold, PHP calls _exit(). This is deliberately not graceful: a clean process death is safer than shutdown handlers walking invalid state.

    Ledger: irrelevant — the worker is gone. The next request gets a fresh process, not a corrupted one.

    The 8.5-specific line

    The fix also adds, after zend_objects_store_free_object_storage() in the shutdown path:

    /* may be used by zend_error_zstr_at before init_executor */
ZVAL_UNDEF(&EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace));

    This is Rule One in its purest form. The request heap is already gone; the array is not freed again. The pointer is simply cleared so the next pre-init error path sees IS_UNDEF and does nothing. On 8.5.12 the producer still creates the backtrace during RSHUTDOWN; the difference is that the global no longer survives with a live-looking type.

    The partial-backport trap

    The advisories state that the complete state reset — not only the OOM change — is required. A partial backport that only replaces longjmp() does not close the stale-state class. The commit is broad because the root cause is a shared failure pattern, not one parser bug: 10,759 additions and 988 deletions across error, session, executor, object, and extension state, plus regression tests for OOM in SplFixedArray, array_fill, array_pad, call frames, debug backtraces, exceptions, interrupts, JSON decoding, and hard-limit compilation failures.

    Go deeper why _exit() is safer, what “safe point” means, and the release matrix

    Why not just catch OOM and continue? Because an OOM can happen at an arbitrary allocation site, including inside a data structure that is halfway through being published. A graceful soft OOM still has to unwind to a point where the state is coherent. PHP defines that point at the VM level, not at every C allocation. The soft OOM is therefore deferred: the failure is recorded, and the VM raises it at the next safe point where the interpreter can abandon the request without violating its own invariants.

    Why _exit() rather than exit()? _exit() bypasses atexit handlers, stdio flushing, and any cleanup that might walk corrupted structures. At the hard limit, the process is not trustworthy enough to run user-space shutdown code. Losing the worker is the intended trade.

    Why fallible allocation matters. emalloc historically bails out on failure. That is convenient for code that assumes success, and disastrous for parsers that are in the middle of publishing state. femalloc and friends return NULL, so the caller can choose to abort the operation, free partial work, and return an error through the normal path. That is what makes “do not faint” implementable at the call sites, not just in the allocator.

    Why the state reset is the bigger half. Removing longjmp() prevents the 109618 bailout corruption, but a worker can still hold a stale pointer for other reasons: a fatal interrupted a reset, or a late diagnostic repopulated error state after the reset. The commit resets error, session, executor, object, and extension state that can be reached during request startup. That is why the fix is large and why the advisory says a partial backport is not complete.

    Release matrix. Fixed releases: PHP 8.2.35, 8.3.36, 8.4.27, 8.5.12. Affected: earlier releases on each branch, with source backports for older EOL branches. The checker treats 8.4.27 and 8.5.12 as the first fixed images for its two probes; it does not claim to prove every variant named in the advisories.

    If you cannot update tonight

    Partial mitigations, ranked by what they actually break.

    The story ELI5 · continuity

    Suppose the hotel cannot replace its checkout procedure yet. It can still make the attack harder, even if it cannot make the desk safe.

    It can refuse unlabeled crates at the front door, by measuring the decoded body instead of trusting a label. It can limit how many folders a guest may bring, and how deeply folders may nest. It can keep the desk smaller, so there is less room to unfold a crate. It can remove the Pink Slip entirely. And it can give each clerk a fresh shift after every guest, so no line survives to be read.

    None of these is the same as fixing the checkout. Each one closes a route to the bad page, but the page is still bad. A determined guest may find another route, and the hotel should still schedule the real repair.

    Mitigations buy time. They do not erase the line.

    Do not do this

    The advisory explicitly warns against using RLIMIT_AS or RLIMIT_DATA as an OOM mitigation. They cause mmap() to fail, which is exactly the path that triggers the vulnerable longjmp(). A lower “limit” can make the bug easier to reach, not harder.

    Logging is an audit tool, not a shield. During an isolated audit, enable log_errors=On, a functional error_log, and error_reporting=E_ALL, then review warnings, deprecations, fatal/parse/uncaught errors, and Maximum execution time exceeded. But the advisory is clear: absence of errors in logs is not proof of safety, and fixing observed errors reduces exposure without replacing the update.

    Go deeper why each mitigation is partial, and how to audit without fooling yourself

    The shape of a partial mitigation. Every item above removes one link: the producer, the consumer, the allocation failure, or the cross-request reuse. The exploit needs all of them. That makes layered mitigations genuinely useful for risk reduction, and it makes any single one of them insufficient as a claim of safety.

    Why memory_limit=-1 is not a fix. It removes the configured ceiling, so ordinary PHP allocations do not fail. But an allocation can still fail below PHP: mmap(), the C library, the kernel, a cgroup, or an address-space limit. If the failure reaches the vulnerable bailout, the state corruption is the same. The advisory lists this as an incomplete mitigation, not a recommendation.

    What to put in front of PHP. Enforce the decoded body size at the reverse proxy or WAF, not just post_max_size. Set a hard maximum on parts, headers per part, field-name depth, and total uploads. If the application never uses multipart, reject multipart/form-data at the edge. These controls shrink the set of requests that can reach the vulnerable startup path.

    Audit, do not infer. A silent warning still touches stale state, so “we saw nothing in the logs” is not evidence. If you are auditing 8.5, turn on the producer, run the negative control, and look for worker PID changes and zend_mm_heap corrupted / AH00052 markers. If you are auditing 8.4, use the checker's paired control; a candidate that merely changes heap layout can look “fixed” for the wrong reason.

    Scope and disclosure. This guide is built from published advisories, the upstream fix commit, and a localhost research harness. The proof command is id; there is no reverse shell, persistence, or destructive payload. The upstream public PoC has no license file and is referenced, not vendored. Do not reuse any of this against systems you do not own.

    The short version

    If you own PHP

    Patch

    Move to 8.2.35, 8.3.36, 8.4.27, 8.5.12 or later. Treat a partial backport of only the longjmp() change as incomplete. Rebuild, restart every worker, and verify with a paired control.

    If you are triaging

    Reduce the routes

    Bound multipart at the edge, disable unused upload progress, set fatal_error_backtraces=0 on 8.5 if you cannot patch, and avoid RLIMIT_AS/RLIMIT_DATA. Then keep pushing the update.

    If you are verifying

    Control first

    A candidate that does not reproduce is only “patched” if a known-vulnerable control did reproduce under the same config. Otherwise the answer is INCONCLUSIVE.

    PHP2ShellThe public research name used by the upstream PoC and this lab. The advisories describe the underlying bugs as OOM corruption (CVE-2026-109618) and stale global state across requests (CVE-2026-109619).
    ZendMMPHP's request allocator. It carves 2 MB chunks from the OS and serves request-owned allocations from pages and size bins inside them.

    zvalPHP's tagged value: refcount, type, and a value union. A zval can hold a string, array, object, reference, or IS_UNDEF.
    zend_stringReference-counted string with a header containing length and hash. A stale string length is what makes the 8.4 leak oversized.
    zend_arrayPHP's hash table. Its header includes arData, element counts, and pDestructor, the function called on each element during destruction.
    pDestructorA function pointer in the zend_array header. If an attacker controls the header, this is the control-flow target. In the 8.4 chain it is set to zif_system.
    zif_systemThe internal implementation of PHP's system(). It expects an execute-data frame and a return-value pointer; the fake array abuses the destructor calling convention to supply a fake frame.
    Bailout / longjmpThe vulnerable OOM exit. It jumps to a catch frame without running C cleanup or destructors, potentially leaving published state inconsistent.
    Soft OOMThe fixed OOM policy: record the failure and raise it at the next VM safe point, so cleanup can finish on a consistent state.
    Hard OOMThe fixed second threshold: _exit() immediately, bypassing shutdown handlers, if memory use is beyond graceful recovery.
    Use-after-free (UAF)Reading, freeing, or branching on memory after its owner has released it. The stale-session and stale-backtrace paths are UAFs.
    ASLRAddress-space layout randomization. The 8.4 leak defeats it by disclosing libphp and the main chunk, so B2 can use absolute addresses.
    Prefork / mod_phpApache's process-per-request model with PHP embedded in the server. Workers are reused; mod_php calls shutdown even after failed startup.
    FPM / FastCGIPHP-FPM speaks FastCGI to a frontend. init_request_info() runs before request startup and exposes an early consumer; FastCGI parameter records cap a value at 65,535 bytes.
    RINIT / RSHUTDOWNModule hooks at request startup and shutdown. Request-owned state is typically created in RINIT and must be cleared by RSHUTDOWN.
    SAPIServer API: mod_php, FPM, FrankenPHP, LSAPI, CLI, etc. The same bug class can have different reachability per SAPI.
    Control / candidateThe known-vulnerable image and the image under test. The control must reproduce before a non-reproducing candidate can be called fixed.
    INCONCLUSIVEThe harness could not attribute a result: broken control, timeout, wrong SAPI, unhealthy target. Never treated as “patched.”

    05

    Five things to remember

    If the details fade, keep these.

    The story the whole guide, compressed
    1. The Ledger outlives the Blotter. Persistent worker state must not point into per-request memory.
    2. OOM is not a normal exit. A longjmp() bailout can skip the cleanup that makes shutdown safe.
    3. Cross-request bugs need a producer and a consumer. The producer writes the stale line; the consumer reads it before initialization can replace it.
    4. A leak turns a crash into control. The 8.4 chain leaks libphp and the main chunk, then forges an array whose destructor is zif_system.
    5. Verify the control, not the claim. If the known-vulnerable image does not reproduce, the answer is INCONCLUSIVE, never PATCHED.

    The fix is to erase the line before you sweep — and to stop fainting while you do it.
